Participants spent a full night in the sleep laboratory with a high-density EEG net applied, and slept undisturbed while the EEG was recorded. The goal of the analysis was to extract each participant's ISFS, and the sections below follow the steps that led to it: who took part (3.1); how the night was recorded, filtered, and cleaned of bad channels and artifactual epochs (3.2); how the continuous artifact-free stretches of N2 sleep were isolated (3.3); how the ISFS was measured within them (3.4–3.5); and how the resulting measures were compared between groups (3.6).

## 3.1 Participants

Data were pooled from two centers, both employing identical high-density EEG setups: one at the Tel Aviv Sourasky Medical Center (TASMC), Tel Aviv, Israel, and one at the CIRUS Centre for Sleep and Chronobiology, Woolcock Institute of Medical Research, Macquarie University, Sydney, Australia. Three groups were analyzed: young healthy controls (n = 35; 27.1 ± 4.3 years, range 21–39; 20 female, 15 male), healthy older adults (n = 39; 66.5 ± 9.7 years, range 49–82; 23 female, 16 male), and patients with amnestic mild cognitive impairment (n = 30; 67.8 ± 9.1 years, range 46–80; 20 female, 10 male).

Healthy participants were recruited from the community. The healthy older adults recorded in Tel Aviv belong to the cohort described in our previous work [@sharon2025slowwaves]; community volunteers whose Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score fell below 26 were not analyzed. The young healthy controls were recruited at TASMC in a separate study and reported no history of neurological, psychiatric, or sleep disorders. At the Sydney site, healthy older adults were recruited [TO SUPPLY: recruitment route and inclusion/exclusion criteria]. Exclusion criteria applied to all participants were any history of sleep apnea, psychiatric or neurological disorder, stroke, or head injury; current alcohol or drug misuse; trans-meridian travel in the week before the recording; and use of hypnotics.

Patients with aMCI recorded in Tel Aviv were recruited through the Memory and Attention Disorders Center of the Neurology Department at TASMC: individuals presenting with subjective cognitive complaints were referred to the clinic's general practitioners, neurologists, geriatricians, and psychiatrists, and those diagnosed with amnestic MCI were invited to take part in the sleep study [@sharon2025slowwaves]. The diagnosis was made clinically at the Cognitive Neurology Unit. Patients who had progressed to a clinical diagnosis of Alzheimer's disease were not included in the present analysis. At the Sydney site, patients with aMCI were recruited and diagnosed [TO SUPPLY: recruitment route, diagnostic criteria, and diagnosing clinician].

Sleep-disordered breathing was assessed in the two older groups. Overnight respiratory monitoring — pulse oximetry, nasal airflow, and thoracic and abdominal effort belts — was reviewed offline by a sleep clinician certified by the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM); the apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) was derived where the recording permitted, and a binary clinical judgment was made otherwise. Participants with moderate or severe obstructive sleep apnea (AHI ≥ 15), central sleep apnea, or hypoventilation were excluded, so that every older participant analyzed here had an AHI of 15 or below [@sharon2025slowwaves].

MoCA scores were obtained during the participants' stay in the sleep laboratory and were available for all of the Tel Aviv participants in the two older groups, but not for the Sydney participants (older adults 27.2 ± 2.6, n = 30; aMCI 21.5 ± 4.3, n = 14). Beyond the diagnostic group criteria, recordings were excluded on technical grounds: more than 20% of electrodes rejected as bad (more than 35 of 176), more than 30% of N2 sleep time rejected as artifactual, fewer than three clean N2 bouts (see Section 3.3), or a total sleep time below 210 min. The 210 min (3.5 h) floor was set so that each analyzed night contained a substantially complete sleep period — more than two full NREM–REM cycles, at the 90–100 min cycle length typical of adults — rather than a fragment of one, so that the N2 entering the analysis was sampled across the night rather than only from its first hours. The per-group exclusion counts are reported in Table 1, which also summarizes the group demographics.

## 3.2 Polysomnographic recordings

High-density PSG was recorded throughout the night at both sites using the same setup: a 256-channel system (Electrical Geodesics, Inc. [EGI]) referenced to Cz, amplified with a NetAmps 300 amplifier and digitized at 1000 Hz, with electrode impedances kept below 50 kΩ. Sleep was scored identically at both sites, in 30 s epochs and according to AASM guidelines, using the sleep module of the Visbrain Python package [@visbrain] within the SleepEEGpy platform [@sleepeegpy]. Scoring was performed on frontal (F3/F4), central (C3/C4), and occipital (O1/O2) derivations referenced to the contralateral mastoid, together with the electrooculogram and the submental electromyogram, and was verified against the spectrogram of the Pz electrode with the hypnogram overlaid. The ISFS analysis was restricted to N2 sleep.

Electrodes overlying the face, neck, and ears, which are not positioned over the scalp and are more prone to muscle and movement artifact, were excluded from analysis, leaving 176 channels for the analysis pipeline.

Recordings were resampled to 250 Hz, notch-filtered at 50 and 100 Hz with a zero-phase FIR filter to remove line noise and its first harmonic, and band-pass filtered between 0.1 and 40 Hz with a zero-phase FIR filter.

Bad channels and artifactual epochs were rejected with a semi-automatic procedure, in which each recording was inspected visually over its full duration. Across subjects, the proportion of channels rejected as bad was 6.3 ± 3.4% in young adults, 1.4 ± 1.8% in older adults, and 3.4 ± 4.4% in aMCI patients, and the proportion of N2 time rejected as artifactual was 4.1 ± 4.9%, 3.3 ± 4.0%, and 1.8 ± 2.0%, respectively (mean ± SD). Data were then re-referenced to the common average reference. The average was computed over the good channels only, excluding channels marked as bad, and those channels were subsequently interpolated from their neighbors.

## 3.3 N2 bout extraction

All epochs not scored as N2 were discarded. Clean N2 bouts were then extracted by splitting each remaining N2 period around any overlapping artifact annotation and retaining only segments of at least 300 s. The 300 s minimum ensures that each bout contains at least two complete cycles of the lowest frequency of interest (0.0075 Hz, period ≈ 133 s). Each retained bout was analyzed independently. A minimum of three clean bouts per subject was required for inclusion; recordings yielding fewer were excluded as having insufficient artifact-free N2 for a stable ISFS estimate. Across the cohort this yielded 1023 clean bouts, a mean of 9.8 per subject (range 3–21).

## 3.4 ISFS feature extraction

ISFS features were computed with a pipeline duplicated and extended from Dimitriades et al. (2024) [@dimitriades2024isfs], adapted here from their 128-channel montage to the 256-channel EGI system. The conceptual steps and a worked single-channel example are shown in Figure 1A–C.

For each clean bout in each channel we extracted the fast-spindle (13–16 Hz) amplitude envelope, subtracted its mean, and applied a Fourier transform to move it into the frequency domain. We then took the power at each frequency, which measures how strongly the envelope fluctuates at that rate, and scaled these values by their own average, so that bouts and channels differing in overall signal strength could be compared on a common scale.

In detail, the envelope was obtained with a Gabor–Morlet wavelet transform, implemented as a frequency-domain convolution via the fast Fourier transform (FFT); wavelets spanned 13.0–16.0 Hz in 0.2 Hz steps with a fixed width of 4 cycles [@tallonbaudry1997gamma], and the magnitude of the resulting complex coefficients gave the time-varying sigma-power envelope. Subtracting the mean of the envelope removes its constant offset, which would otherwise place the envelope's entire average level in the 0 Hz bin and dominate the low-frequency end of the spectrum. The spectrum itself is the squared magnitude of the Fourier coefficients, resampled onto a frequency axis common to all bouts so that bouts of different length could be averaged, and each bout spectrum was then divided by its own mean power. If the spectral maximum fell within the lowest frequency bins (0–0.006 Hz), those bins were discarded to suppress residual low-frequency drift.

The normalized bout spectra were then averaged within each channel. Such an averaged spectrum does not sit on a flat floor: broadband noise adds an offset that varies from channel to channel and that would inflate any peak measured on top of it. We therefore estimated that offset from the 0.06–0.10 Hz range, which lies above the ISFS band and so contains no ISFS signal, and subtracted it from the whole spectrum, leaving a flat floor against which a peak can be measured.

To characterize the ISFS we needed three numbers per channel: how fast the rhythm runs, how sharply that rate is defined, and how strong the rhythm is. We obtained all three by fitting a bell-shaped curve to the peak of the baseline-corrected mean spectrum and reading the values off the fit, as illustrated in Figure 1C. The curve was a Gaussian, *a*·exp(−((*f*−*b*)/*c*)²), fitted by nonlinear least squares, and the three parameters were read from it as **peak frequency** (the frequency of the fitted maximum), **bandwidth** (the width of the *b* ± |*c*| band), and **AUC** (the integral of the fitted Gaussian over *b* ± |*c*|). A fit was accepted only if its peak rose above a detection threshold set at 1.5 times the standard deviation of the spectrum, its peak frequency fell inside the ISFS band (0.0075–0.04 Hz), and the numerically integrated area under the peak was positive, the last condition rejecting degenerate fits. Channels whose fit failed any of these checks were treated as having no measurable ISFS and left missing.

## 3.5 Region of interest

A central-parietal region of interest (ROI) was defined a priori from the young-adult AUC hotspot reported by Dimitriades et al. (2024) [@dimitriades2024isfs]. Their 128-channel hotspot was mapped onto the 256-channel EGI layout by spatial correspondence, giving a fixed set of central-parietal electrodes (Figure 1D). The ROI was fixed before any group comparison, was identical for all subjects and groups, and was used specifically to compare the AUC parameter across the three groups (Section 3.6).

## 3.6 Statistical analysis

Group comparisons were performed at three levels of spatial detail: averaged across the whole scalp, mapped channel by channel as topographies, and summarized within the pre-defined region of interest.

**Whole-scalp.** For each ISFS parameter, each subject contributed a single value: the mean across all channels with an accepted fit. Per-group normality and variance homogeneity were checked with Shapiro–Wilk and Levene's tests. Peak frequency, bandwidth, and the normalized ROI value met these assumptions and were compared with one-way ANOVAs, with effect size reported as η²; whole-scalp AUC did not and was compared with a Kruskal–Wallis test. Where the omnibus test was significant, post-hoc pairwise comparisons used Tukey's HSD (Dunn's test with Holm correction was the non-parametric counterpart, which was not required here).

**Topographic.** Spatial group differences were tested with cluster-based permutation tests (MNE-Python [@gramfort2013mnepython], following Maris and Oostenveld [@maris2007nonparametric]). Channel adjacency was derived from the EGI 256-channel montage. The three-group analysis used an F-statistic with the cluster-forming threshold set at the p = 0.05 F-critical value (one-tailed), 5000 permutations. Tukey–Kramer post-hoc tests were run at each electrode of any significant cluster to localize the pairwise differences.

**ROI and normalization.** For the ROI analysis and for normalized topographies, each subject's per-channel values were first normalized by that subject's whole-scalp mean of the metric, and only then restricted to the ROI; normalizing within the ROI was deliberately avoided because it forces the ROI mean toward 1 by construction. The normalized AUC value per subject was the mean over the ROI channels, compared across groups with the same omnibus and post-hoc scheme as the whole-scalp analysis.

**Demographics and sleep architecture.** Age and MoCA were compared between the two older groups with a Welch t-test, or a Mann–Whitney U test where normality failed. Sleep-stage proportions were compared across the three groups with the same omnibus and post-hoc scheme as the ISFS parameters.

## 3.7 Software

Analyses used Python with MNE-Python [@gramfort2013mnepython], NumPy, SciPy, pandas, and statsmodels. YASA [@vallat2021yasa] was used only to detect the spindles illustrated in Figure 1A and was not part of the ISFS analysis itself.
